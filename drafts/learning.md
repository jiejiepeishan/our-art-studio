Because you mentioned you already own Hansa Yellow (likely Hansa Yellow Medium or Light), buying M. Graham's Azo Yellow (PY151) would be highly redundant. They serve the exact same primary mixing role on a palette. 



However, there are a few subtle behavioral differences you should know when swapping them:

\## 1. Transparency and Glazing **#we should add this type of note explaining how the colors behave into Ace's note in each color card.**

\* Azo Yellow (Benzimidazolone - PY151): This pigment is highly transparent to semi-transparent. It is prized by artists who do heavy layering or glazing because it doesn't get chalky when stacked in multiple washes.

\* Hansa Yellow (Arylide - PY97 or PY3): Hansa yellows are naturally semi-transparent. They have a tiny bit more physical "body" to them. If you apply Hansa yellow in very thick, concentrated layers, it can look a tiny bit more opaque on the paper than Azo yellow. 





\## 2. Hue Balance (The Temperature) **#we should add this type of note explaining color temperature and color shade into Ace's note in each color card.**

\* Hansa Yellow Medium (PY97): This is widely considered a "perfect" neutral primary yellow. It doesn't lean aggressively green (cool) or orange (warm). 

\* Azo Yellow (PY151): Depending on the brand, Azo Yellow often leans just a fraction cooler (slightly greener undertone) than a true Hansa Medium. 





Q3: then can burnt sienna and raw ember kinda substitute each other?

A3:No, Burnt Sienna and Raw Umber cannot substitute for each other. They are complete polar opposites on the color wheel of browns, and swapping them will drastically change your mixing results. 

Even though M. Graham makes both colors using variations of the same earth pigment class (PBr7), their processing and undertones are completely different: 

\## 1. Color Temperature \& Undertones

\* Burnt Sienna (020): This is a heavily heat-roasted (calcined) clay. The baking process turns it into a warm, fiery, glowing reddish-orange brown. Think of it as a highly muted, rustic orange. **#I love this kind of explanation on the pigment, can we add these in "Ace's Art History Bite"?** 

\* Raw Umber (170): This is the raw, unbaked earth that contains natural manganese. It is a deep, dark, very cool-toned espresso brown with a distinct greenish/yellowish undertone. **#I love this kind of explanation on the pigment, can we add these in "Ace's Art History Bite"?**



\## 2. Their Roles in Mixing (Completely Different!) **#This part is very important on teaching me what to pick and how to use them. Can we find a way to integrate this information in mixing wheel or somewhere under Kits tab? Let's discuss this first?**

If you try to swap them in your mixing recipes, your colors will clash:

| Attribute / Mix | Burnt Sienna (Warm / Red-Orange) | Raw Umber (Cool / Green-Brown) |

|---|---|---|

| Mixed with Ultramarine Blue | Makes a beautiful, balanced neutral gray or rich black. | Makes a very slate-like, icy, cool dark teal-gray. |

| Mixed with your Greens | Tones them down into beautiful, warm, earthy autumn ochres or olive moss. | Darkens them into cool, deep, moody pine forest shadows. |

| Skin Tones \& Sunsets | Excellent for warming up portraits or painting rich, glowing desert rocks. | Will make skin tones look sickly, muddy, or bruised due to the green undertone. |



\## Summary: Keep Both on Your Palette!

In watercolor, Burnt Sienna acts as your warm dark (or desaturated orange), while Raw Umber acts as your cool dark (or desaturated yellow-green). Because they sit on opposite sides of the temperature spectrum, they work together as a team rather than replacing one another. Keep both!
